When the Capacitive–Faradaic Split Holds — and When It Fails

A first-principles benchmark of the scan-rate decomposition against Butler–Volmer kinetics and semi-infinite diffusion, mapping where it returns physically meaningful coefficients and where it invents them.

Background

Battery or capacitor? The answer hides in one current

Deconvoluting faradaic (redox) from non-faradaic (capacitive) current is a central challenge in electrochemical energy storage.

Both currents overlap across the same potential window and cannot be distinguished by visual inspection. Yet their ratio determines whether a material behaves as a battery or a capacitor, and sets the trade-off between energy and power density. Cyclic voltammetry is the conventional experimental choice for this deconvolution: it is fast, and it probes the electrode response across timescales through variation of the scan rate.

The current–potential relationship from CV encodes the thermodynamics of the charge-transfer reaction together with the kinetics and mass transport in a single measurement. That information density reveals a great deal about the material — but it is also what makes separating the currents difficult, and the theoretical frameworks for the separation remain underdeveloped.

The Standard Method

The scan-rate decomposition

Lacking a tractable physical framework, the field converged on an empirical decomposition. Dunn and coworkers (2007) express the current at each potential as a sum of two power laws in scan rate and fit the coefficients by ordinary least squares, separately at every potential, across a range of scan rates:

The faradaic ν1/2 term traces to Randles–Ševčík: for a fully reversible reaction coupled to semi-infinite diffusion, the peak current scales as the square root of scan rate.

The capacitive ν term reflects the observation that double-layer charging scales linearly with scan rate. Lindström (1997) formalized the power-law ansatz i ∝ νb, with the exponent read as a diagnostic: b = 0.5 for diffusion control, b = 1 for surface confinement.

The catch: Randles–Ševčík's ν1/2 holds only at the peak, and only in the reversible limit. Extending it across the whole potential window and the entire reversibility continuum is an extrapolation adopted without first-principles justification — and Eq. (1) assumes k1 is constant, while in practice it drifts with potential.

The Failure

Two failures: one physical, one statistical

Benchmarked against an exact Butler–Volmer + diffusion simulation, the decomposition returns physically faithful coefficients only in a narrow, reversible corner — while reporting R2 ≈ 1 everywhere. Two distinct issues underlie the breakdown.

1 — The physics: b is not 0.5

The true scan-rate exponent is a continuous, potential-dependent function b(E). It approaches the ideal diffusive value 0.5 only in the highly reversible limit; as reversibility decreases, b falls continuously below 0.5. A single fixed exponent cannot represent a continuously varying kinetic response, so the assumed ν1/2 over-predicts the faradaic term wherever kinetics are sluggish.

2 — The statistics: least squares hides it

Ordinary least squares minimizes the absolute residual, so it fits the largest signal — the faradaic current — most accurately. The small faradaic scaling mismatch is then absorbed into the k1 ν capacitive term. When the faradaic current is an order of magnitude larger than the capacitive one, that mismatch is amplified on the way in: a tiny faradaic error becomes a large spurious capacitance. The error is largest exactly where the true capacitance is small and kinetics are slow — the regime where a capacitive assignment is least justified and most often invoked.

A high R2 tells you the sum was reproduced. It tells you nothing about whether the split was right.

This is the crux, because in practice R2 is exactly the criterion the field leans on. For a real material there is no independent ground truth — no separately known capacitive and faradaic currents to check the split against — so a near-perfect fit is taken as the licence to trust the reported fractions. On our synthetic and blank-subtracted systems we do have that ground truth, and it shows the licence is unearned: the fit reproduces the total current while assigning the parts wrongly. Where a check is possible the split fails, and where the method is actually used no check is available.

The Scaling Theory

One number governs every point: the local Damköhler number

The structure of b(E) is not arbitrary. Starting from the exact Volterra equation of sweep voltammetry and freezing the memory integral over the elapsed time t̃ = (ηi − η)/σ, the theory produces a single local Damköhler number — the ratio of Butler–Volmer reaction velocity to the diffusive supply velocity of the locally grown layer — that controls every point of the voltammogram:

Overlay contours of constant Da on the measured b(E, Λ) map and the color bands run parallel to the iso-Da contours, not to either axis: the pointwise exponent collapses onto the local Damköhler number, not the single wave-level ψ.

A caveat worth stating plainly: Eq. (4) is an approximation, obtained by freezing the memory integral at each potential (a quasi-steady step). It therefore does not capture the full memory of the sweep — the way the current at one potential depends on the entire depletion history that preceded it — nor the cycle-to-cycle changes of a multi-cycle experiment, where each vertex resets the diffusion layer. What Eq. (5) gives is a master curve; the true, simulated exponent scatters as a perturbation around it rather than lying exactly on it. The claim is that Da organizes b to leading order, not that it fixes it to the last digit.

Explore · the statistician's dilemma

Widen the window, break the physics

The two basis functions are collinear: over a narrow scan-rate window the columns ν and ν1/2 are nearly proportional, so the condition number and variance-inflation factor (VIF) explode and the k1/k2 split is undetermined. Widening the range relieves that — but reaches scan rates where the true exponent b varies widely, so the single-ν1/2 model is more badly mis-specified. Drag the handles and watch both sides of the trade move at once.

The fit is just an ordinary regression of the measured current i on two predictors — ν and ν½ — because the model is i = k1ν + k2√ν; those two columns are the only knobs the fit can turn. corr(ν, ν½) asks how alike those two knobs behave across the scan rates you use, r = Σ(ν−ν̄)(√ν−√ν̄) ⁄ √[Σ(ν−ν̄)² · Σ(√ν−√ν̄)²]. Over a narrow window ν and √ν rise together almost identically, so r → 1 and the fit cannot tell k1ν apart from k2√ν — it can shift current from one to the other with no change to their sum. The variance-inflation factor puts a number on that ambiguity, VIF = 1 ⁄ (1 − r²) (VIF = 1 independent; VIF ≫ 10 means the split is badly determined). Δb is how much the true exponent drifts across the window; the single-ν½ model is only valid when Δb is small.

Errors in the Literature

The pathology is already in published data

These failures are not hypothetical. Pilon and coworkers showed by first-principles simulation that a b-value of unity can arise from purely faradaic charge storage in thin films with fast intercalation — directly contradicting the standard "b = 1 ⇒ capacitive" reading. Costentin demonstrated explicit cases where the decomposition returns physically inconsistent results and argued for case-specific physical models. Bond drew attention to the broader pattern and called for physics-based frameworks to replace empirical correlators.

The signature of a failure is an unphysical coefficient. Applying the standard procedure to the canonical Li-intercalation-in-anatase data returns a negative k1 — a capacitive current pointing the wrong way. Adding the peak-alignment pre-processing step the original analysis mentions makes it worse: the number of unphysical fits grows, with negative k1 and k2 appearing at additional potentials. A second material, BaCoO3, reports a mixture of positive, negative, and sign-flipped k1, k2 across the scan-rate direction. The split is sensitive not only to the assumed physics but to seemingly minor data-processing choices.

Experiment

Even ferrocene fails

Does a real, well-behaved system live in that reversible corner? Ferrocene in TBAPF6/acetonitrile is about as ideal as electrochemistry gets: a fast, chemically reversible one-electron couple on a planar electrode, peak separation 60–80 mV. Recording the blank electrolyte supplies the capacitive current directly, and the subtraction iferrocene − iblank supplies the faradaic current — so the decomposition can be judged against measured ground truth.

Against that ground truth the split is unphysical: the capacitive term overstates the measured blank roughly tenfold with the wrong shape (R2 = −597, worse than a constant); the diffusive coefficient takes the wrong sign at 55% of potentials; the assigned capacitive fraction is 40% where the measured value is 4%. The overall fit looks unremarkable (median R2 = 0.72, cond = 118, VIF = 14.5) — fit quality is decoupled from partition accuracy.

That this happens for ferrocene is the strongest form of the argument. The failure is not a sluggish couple, a resistive cell, a porous electrode, or overlapping redox — none of those are present. It arises because the fixed {ν, ν1/2} basis is mis-specified for any real interface: the measured double-layer current scales as να with α ≈ 0.67 (constant-phase behavior), not ν, and the faradaic current follows a potential-dependent exponent rather than a clean ν1/2.

Key insight

If the decomposition invents capacitance on ferrocene — where the true value is independently known to be small — the fractions it reports for disordered pseudocapacitive materials, where no ground truth exists, cannot be regarded as physically meaningful.

The Way Forward

Fit the physics, not the power law

The diagnostics motivate a physics-based inversion. Rather than fitting two collinear power laws point-by-point, the measured voltammograms are described by their governing forward model — the full Butler–Volmer / semi-infinite-diffusion system — and the physical parameters (k0, Cdl, transport constants) are recovered directly by simultaneous inversion across all scan rates and both branches, with identifiability assessed from the parameter covariance. No scan-rate scaling assumption is invoked; the recovered parameters stay physically consistent because the model that generated the data is the model being fit.
